📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Two factors, one score each. Two categorical variables sort the same people at the same time, and every person gives exactly one number. That is the whole design.
  2. Levels × levels = cells. The name of the design IS the multiplication. A 3 × 2 has 6 cells; a 3 × 4 has 12. Count the cells before you do anything else — almost every df needs that number.
  3. Three questions, three F values. Main effect of A, main effect of B, interaction A × B. One analysis, one table, three separate verdicts.
  4. Main effects live in the MARGINS; the interaction lives in the CELLS. A main effect compares row means or column means. The interaction is about the pattern of the four (or six, or twelve) cell means.
  5. All the spread splits, and then splits again. SS Total = SS Between + SS Within. And SS Between = SS(A) + SS(B) + SS(A × B). SS Within never splits.
  6. Every df is “minus one” — except the interaction, which MULTIPLIES. df(A) = levels of A − 1. df(B) = levels of B − 1. df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B). df Within = N − cells. df Total = N − 1.
  7. Divide ACROSS the row, compare DOWN the column. MS = SS ÷ df on the same row, never across rows. Then every F = that MS ÷ the ONE MS Within.
  8. An F is meaningless without its pair of df. Always F(effect df, error df) — the effect’s own df first, the error df second. The two orders have completely different critical values.
  9. Parallel means no interaction. In a plot of cell means: parallel lines → no interaction; non-parallel, including crossing → interaction. Crossing is just the extreme case.
  10. Read the interaction FIRST. If it is significant, the main effects can no longer be read at face value and you must describe the cell means. This is the lecture’s own caution, and it is where exam marks hide.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • Recognise a two-factor design from a description, and name its factors, levels and cells.
  • State the three null hypotheses a two-factor ANOVA tests.
  • Explain what a main effect is, and why “main” does not mean “most important”.
  • Define an interaction in two equivalent ways: an unanticipated combination, and an effect that differs across the levels of another factor.
  • Split SS Between into SS(A), SS(B) and SS(A × B), and compute each one from a table of means.
  • Split the degrees of freedom the same way, including df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B).
  • Complete a two-factor ANOVA table and compute all three F values against a shared MS Within.
  • Compare each F with its critical value and state a p range, a decision and a conclusion in words.
  • Read an interaction plot: parallel means no interaction, non-parallel means interaction.
  • Say which conclusions a design licenses — causal for a manipulated factor, associational for an observed one.
  • List the advantages of a two-factor design, including the one no series of one-way ANOVAs can deliver.
  • Describe the Dove, Byrne &amp; Bruce (2009) findings and explain why the interaction is the whole story.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

A two-factor ANOVA takes the machine you already know and splits its between-groups part into three. Two factors sort people into cells; the spread of those cell means around the grand mean is SS Between, and it divides into SS(A), SS(B) and whatever is left over, which is the interaction. The degrees of freedom divide the same way, with the interaction df a product rather than a sum. Each piece becomes a mean square and is divided by one shared MS Within, giving three F values tested against the same table. The two main effects ask whether each factor matters on its own, averaging the other away; the interaction asks whether the effect of one depends on the level of the other, which is the one question a stack of separate one-way ANOVAs can never answer. And it is the question that usually matters most: in the lecture’s own example fandom has no main effect at all, and yet it decides whether the music adds two points or twenty-two — exactly as, in Dove, Byrne and Bruce (2009), a thinking style with no main effect on depression decides whether depression tracks BMI at all. Which is why the rule is: plot the cell means, read the interaction first, and only then tell a story about the main effects.

1step

🎯 Start with the questions

Why a second factor, and what it buys you

⬇ builds onNothing yet — but it assumes you already know the one-factor ANOVA from Theme 6.⬆ hands upThree research questions instead of one: factor A, factor B, and the interaction.
  • ▸MAIN EFFECT OF A — does factor A change the score, averaging over all the levels of B?
  • ▸MAIN EFFECT OF B — does factor B change the score, averaging over all the levels of A?
  • ▸INTERACTION A × B — does the effect of A DEPEND on which level of B you are looking at?
🪝 memory hookColumns, rows, pattern. Three questions, three F values, one table.

In words

Last week one grouping variable sorted people and you asked one question: are these means different? This week two grouping variables sort the same people at the same time. Nothing about the DATA changes — one quantitative score per person, categorical factors — but the between-groups variation now splits into three named pieces instead of staying whole, and that third piece is the reason the design exists.

  • All three come out of ONE analysis and ONE table. You do not run three separate studies.
  • The interaction is the decisive one: a series of one-way ANOVAs can never test it.

Rules of the game for step 1

  • One quantitative score per person, two categorical factors. That is the whole design.
  • “One-way” meant one factor. “Two-way” means two — not two levels.
  • A main effect means that factor ON ITS OWN, with the other averaged away. “Main” does not mean “most important”.

Say it to a friend

“Last week I compared three kinds of music on one score. This week there are two things sorting people at once — the music AND whether they’re a fan — and one analysis tells me about each of them separately plus whether they depend on each other.”

Key insight

Adding a second factor does not add a second analysis. It splits the between-groups variation you already had into three named pieces: factor A, factor B, and the interaction between them.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading “main effect” as “the important one”. A factor with no main effect at all can be the most interesting thing in the study — which is exactly what happens in this lecture’s own example.

The three questions, side by side

🧪 Demo 1 — one factor, two factors, or neither?
Pick one. You get the reasoning either way.

Check yourself

A study measures exam score across four teaching methods, and also records whether each student is a first-year or a second-year, comparing all of those groups in one analysis. One factor or two?
Two: teaching method (4 levels) and year (2 levels). That is a 4 × 2 two-factor ANOVA, with 8 cells.
What would “no interaction” mean, in words?
The effect of one factor is the same size at every level of the other. Whatever the teaching method does, it does equally to first-years and second-years.

🏗️ Name the design

Factors, levels, cells — and the numbers they produce

⬇ builds onThe three questions from step 1.⬆ hands upa, b, the number of cells and N. Every degree of freedom is built from these.
  • ▸FACTOR — one of the two categorical variables. The lecture calls them Sound and Fan.
  • ▸LEVEL — one category of one factor. Sound has 2 levels; Fan has 2 levels.
  • ▸CELL — one combination of a level of A with a level of B. 2 × 2 = 4 cells.
🪝 memory hookLevels times levels equals cells. The name of the design IS the multiplication.

In words

Before anything is computed you have to read the design off the page. Three words do all the work — factor, level, cell — and the name of the design is literally the multiplication.

  • The design is named by its levels: this is a 2 × 2. A 2 × 3 would have 6 cells.
  • BOTH factors are between-subjects: every person sits in exactly ONE cell and gives ONE score.

Rules of the game for step 2

  • cells = levels of A × levels of B. Count the cells before anything else — almost every df needs that number.
  • N = cells × people per cell. The per-cell number is not N.
  • Find the measured NUMBER first: that is the response variable, and it is never a factor.

Say it to a friend

“The design is a grid. One factor runs across the top, the other down the side, and every box in the grid is a different group of people.”

Key insight

Every person contributes one score to exactly one cell. That single sentence is what makes this a between-subjects design — and it is what the whole degrees-of-freedom bookkeeping depends on.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Offering the LEVELS as the factors. “Men and women” is one factor, gender, with two levels. And a combination of levels is a cell, not a factor.

The lecture’s own study

Check yourself

A study crosses three doses of caffeine with two times of day, 8 people per combination. Name the design, the cells and N.
A 3 × 2 two-factor design, with 6 cells and N = 6 × 8 = 48.
Thirty people each do the task once with music and once in silence. Is that this kind of design?
No. Each person gives two scores, so the groups are not independent — that is a within-subject design, and today’s ANOVA assumes one score per person.

🔬 Check the level of control

Which factor you may speak causally about — decided before any arithmetic

⬇ builds onThe named factors from step 2.⬆ hands upA licence, or the absence of one, for each factor separately.
  • ▸SOUND was manipulated: the researcher decided who heard what, and assignment was random.
  • ▸FAN was not manipulated: nobody can be randomly assigned to love Michael Jackson.
  • ▸So the study is EXPERIMENTAL with respect to Sound and PASSIVELY OBSERVED with respect to Fan.
🪝 memory hookManipulated and randomised → cause. Brought with them → association.

In words

The lecture makes a point of this early, and exams love it. In this study one factor was manipulated by the researcher with random assignment; the other was not — people arrived already sorted. That difference changes nothing about the arithmetic and everything about the sentence you are allowed to write at the end.

  • For Sound you may say the music CAUSED the change. For Fan you may only say the groups DIFFER.
  • Read the interaction in the direction of the factor you may speak causally about.

Rules of the game for step 3

  • Causal licence is decided factor by factor, not study by study.
  • It takes BOTH: manipulated, and randomly assigned. Either missing → association only.
  • The statistics are identical either way. Only the wording of the conclusion changes.

Say it to a friend

“We decided what music people heard, so we can blame the music for the difference. We didn’t decide who’s a fan, so for that part all we can say is that fans and non-fans scored differently — not why.”

Key insight

A two-factor ANOVA happily mixes an experimental factor and an observed one. Doing this check at step 3, before you compute anything, means you already know what kind of sentence you are allowed to end with.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Letting a large F buy you a causal claim. School type, political party, gender, fandom — people arrive already in those groups, and no F however large licenses causality there.

🧪 Demo 2 — what may you claim, factor by factor?

Check yourself

The interaction turns out significant. How should you word it?
Prefer the direction where the manipulated factor is doing the acting: “the effect of the sound depends on whether the listener is a fan”. Saying the music changed who is a fan would be the wrong way round.
Would the arithmetic differ if BOTH factors had been manipulated?
No. The sums of squares, df and F values are computed identically. Only the causal language in your conclusion would change.

🔢 Read the table of means

Four kinds of mean, and which question each one answers

⬇ builds onThe grid from step 2 — you now know what each cell contains.⬆ hands upThe cell, row, column and grand means that every sum of squares is built from.
  • ▸CELL means — the four raw averages: 20, 22, 18 and 40. The interaction lives here.
  • ▸ROW means (marginal) — not a fan 21, fan 29. These answer the main effect of FAN.
  • ▸COLUMN means (marginal) — weather 19, Michael 31. These answer the main effect of SOUND.
🪝 memory hookCells for the interaction, margins for the main effects, grand mean for everything.

In words

The lecture prints the data as a 2 × 2 table of cell means and variances, with the row means, column means and grand mean sitting in the margins. Learning to read that table is half of this theme, because every sum of squares is built out of it.

  • GRAND mean — 25. Every “between” deviation is measured from this one number.
  • The cell variances (79, 81, 78, 82) are used only for SS Within — the noise.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • Main effects live in the MARGINS; the interaction lives in the CELLS.
  • A marginal mean deliberately throws information away — 29 for the fans mixes a cell mean of 18 with one of 40.
  • Write the grand mean down once and reuse it. Every between-groups deviation is measured from it.

Say it to a friend

“The four numbers in the middle of the table are the actual group averages. The numbers down the side and along the bottom are those averages collapsed one way or the other, and they’re what the two main effects compare.”

Key insight

That a marginal mean hides information is not a flaw — it is the whole point of a main effect. It only becomes a problem when the interaction is significant, which is why you check that first.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Averaging the two cell means to get a row mean without checking the cell sizes. It works here only because both cells hold the same NUMBER of people; with unequal cells you must weight them.

Check yourself

Check the row mean for the fans from the cell means.
(18 + 40) ⁄ 2 = 29. It works as a simple average because both cells hold five people each.
Which means would you describe FIRST if the interaction is significant?
The cell means. With an interaction present, the row and column means hide the very thing you need to describe.

0️⃣ Write the three hypotheses

Two familiar nulls, and one with a different shape

⬇ builds onThe named factors and their levels from step 2.⬆ hands upThe three claims the three F tests are about to put on trial.
  • ▸H₀ for A: the population means for the levels of A are equal — the column means do not differ.
  • ▸H₀ for B: the population means for the levels of B are equal — the row means do not differ.
  • ▸H₀ for A × B: there is NO interaction — the effect of A is the same at every level of B.
🪝 memory hookTwo “are the means equal?” and one “does it depend?”.

In words

Three questions means three null hypotheses, and in an exam you may be asked to write all three. Two of them look exactly like the ones you already know. The third is different in shape, and it is the one people get wrong.

  • Every alternative is non-directional: “they are not equal”, “there is an interaction”.
  • The three decisions are independent: you may reject none, one, two or all three.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • Write all three down before you compute anything. It stops you reporting the wrong F against the wrong claim.
  • The interaction null is about DIFFERENCES being equal, not about means being equal.
  • There is no one-tailed version of any of them.

Say it to a friend

“Three claims to knock down: the music does nothing, being a fan does nothing, and the two of them don’t depend on each other.”

Key insight

The interaction null is not “the cell means are all equal” — that would be far stronger. It says only that the GAP produced by A among the B₁ people equals the gap produced by A among the B₂ people.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing the interaction null as “all four cell means are equal”. That claims there are no effects at all, which is a different and much bigger claim.

Check yourself

Write H₀ for the main effect of Sound in words.
In the population, the mean spatial awareness score is the same for people who hear the weather report and people who hear Michael Jackson, averaged over fans and non-fans.
Can the alternative hypothesis for an interaction be one-sided?
No. Like every ANOVA alternative it simply says the null is false — here, that an interaction exists. The F test is right-tailed, which is a different thing.

✂️ Split the variation

The between-groups part divides into three

⬇ builds onThe means from step 4 and the hypotheses from step 5.⬆ hands upThe structure the whole analysis rests on — and the subtraction that finds the interaction.
  • ▸SS Total = SS Between + SS Within. Unchanged from the one-factor ANOVA.
  • ▸SS Between now splits three ways: SS(A) + SS(B) + SS(A × B).
  • ▸SS Within does NOT split. It is still just “people differ from others in their own cell”.
🪝 memory hookTotal splits in two; Between splits in three. Within never splits.

In words

Here is the one structural idea of the lecture. The total spread still splits into Between and Within exactly as it did last week — but now the Between part splits again, into the two main effects and the interaction.

  • In this study: 1540 = 320 + 720 + 500, and 1540 + 1280 = 2820.
  • The interaction is obtained by SUBTRACTION — it is what the two main effects could not explain.

Rules of the game for step 6

  • Only the BETWEEN part splits. Within stays whole — it is the yardstick.
  • The three pieces must add back to SS Between exactly. That is your free check.
  • You never need a direct formula for the interaction. It is always a remainder.

Say it to a friend

“All the variation in the scores gets divided up. Some of it is because of the music, some because of fandom, some because of the particular combination, and the rest is just people being different.”

Key insight

The interaction sum of squares is a residual INSIDE the between-groups part: the variation among the cells that the two factors acting separately cannot account for. That is why it never needs a formula of its own.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Expecting SS Within to split too. It does not. In a two-factor ANOVA it is still one number, and it is the denominator of all three tests.

Check yourself

SS Between = 900, SS(A) = 400, SS(B) = 100. What is SS(A × B)?
900 − 400 − 100 = 400.
Your three between pieces add to more than SS Between. What has gone wrong?
One of the pieces is wrong — most often because a row or column mean was weighted by the wrong n. The three must add to SS Between exactly.

🧮 Compute each sum of squares

The same sum, four times over

⬇ builds onThe split from step 6, and the means from step 4.⬆ hands upSS(A), SS(B), SS(A × B) and SS Within — four numbers.
  • ▸SS Between = Σ n(cell mean − grand)² = 5(20−25)² + 5(22−25)² + 5(18−25)² + 5(40−25)² = 1540.
  • ▸SS(Fan) = Σ n(row mean − grand)² = 10(21−25)² + 10(29−25)² = 320.
  • ▸SS(Sound) = Σ n(column mean − grand)² = 10(19−25)² + 10(31−25)² = 720.
🪝 memory hookMean minus grand mean, squared, times its own n. Four times over.

In words

Four sums, and they are the same sum four times: take a mean, subtract the grand mean, square it, multiply by how many people are behind that mean, add them all up. Only the words “cell”, “row” and “column” change.

  • SS Within = Σ (n − 1) × cell variance = 4(79) + 4(81) + 4(78) + 4(82) = 1280.
  • SS(Interaction) = 1540 − 320 − 720 = 500, and SS Total = 1540 + 1280 = 2820.

Rules of the game for step 7

  • The n in each line is the number of people BEHIND that mean: 5 for a cell, 10 for a row or column here.
  • SS Within comes from the cell VARIANCES, never from the means — which is why no effect can touch it.
  • The three between pieces add to SS Between; Between plus Within adds to Total. Check both.

Say it to a friend

“For each average I work out how far it sits from the overall average, square it so the minus signs don’t cancel, and weight it by how many people that average was built from.”

Key insight

The weight is always the number of observations that mean was computed from. That single sentence resolves every “is it 5 or 10?” hesitation in the whole calculation.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Using 5 for the row and column means. Each of those is the average of TEN people — two cells of five — so the weight is 10.

🧪 Demo 3 — split the sums of squares yourself
Four cell means, four cell variances, equal cell sizes. The lecture’s own numbers are loaded.

Check yourself

Why is the multiplier 10 and not 5 for the row means?
Because each row mean is the average of ten people — two cells of five. The weight is always the number of observations that mean was computed from.
Compute SS Within if the four cell variances were 60, 64, 58 and 62 with n = 5 each.
4(60 + 64 + 58 + 62) = 4 × 244 = 976.

📐 Split the degrees of freedom

Everything is “minus one” — except the interaction, which multiplies

⬇ builds ona, b and N from step 2, and the SS split from step 6.⬆ hands upFour degrees of freedom, and the pair each F will be reported with.
  • ▸df Between = number of CELLS − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3.
  • ▸df(A) = levels of A − 1 = 1. df(B) = levels of B − 1 = 1.
  • ▸df(A × B) = df(A) × df(B) — a PRODUCT, not a sum. Here 1 × 1 = 1.
🪝 memory hookEverything is “minus one”, except the interaction, which multiplies.

In words

The degrees of freedom split exactly the way the sums of squares do — which is handy, because it gives you a free check. One of these four rules is unlike the others, and it is the one worth memorising.

  • df Within = N − number of cells = 20 − 4 = 16.
  • Check: 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 = df Between, and 3 + 16 = 19 = N − 1.

Rules of the game for step 8

  • df(A × B) is a product. This is the single most commonly dropped mark in the theme.
  • df Within is N minus the number of CELLS, never N − 1 or N − 2.
  • All four must add to N − 1. If they do not, one of them is wrong.

Say it to a friend

“Each factor loses one degree of freedom for its own levels, the interaction gets the two leftovers multiplied together, and whatever’s left over belongs to the people.”

Key insight

In a 2 × 2 all three effect df equal 1, which makes it a flattering example: adding and multiplying give the same answer. Do one calculation on a 2 × 3 before the exam, where df(A × B) = 1 × 2 = 2 and the rule stops hiding behind ones.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Adding the two main-effect df instead of multiplying them. In a 2 × 2 it is invisible; in a 2 × 3 it costs you the mark.

🧪 Demo 4 — the degrees-of-freedom machine

Check yourself

A 3 × 3 design with 45 people. Give all the df.
df A = 2, df B = 2, df A×B = 2 × 2 = 4, df Within = 45 − 9 = 36, df Total = 44. Check: 2 + 2 + 4 + 36 = 44 ✓
Someone writes df(A × B) = df(A) + df(B). How would you catch it?
The three between pieces would no longer add to df Between. In a 2 × 3 the sum would be 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, but df Between is only 6 − 1 = 5.

9step

⚖️ Build the table — three F values

One MS Within, three signals

⬇ builds onThe sums of squares from step 7 and the degrees of freedom from step 8.⬆ hands upThree F statistics, each with its own pair of df.
  • ▸MS = SS ÷ df, computed along the row, exactly as in a one-factor ANOVA.
  • ▸MS Within = 1280 ⁄ 16 = 80. This is the denominator of all three F values.
  • ▸Fan: MS = 320, F = 320 ⁄ 80 = 4.00, reported as F(1, 16) = 4.00.
🪝 memory hookDivide along the row to get MS; divide by MS Within to get F. Three times.

In words

Now everything goes into one table. Each effect gets its own mean square by dividing along its own row, and then each F is that mean square divided by the SAME MS Within. One noise estimate; three signals.

  • Sound: MS = 720, F = 720 ⁄ 80 = 9.00. Interaction: MS = 500, F = 500 ⁄ 80 = 6.25.
  • Every F is written with its own numerator df first and the shared df Within second.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • Divide ACROSS the row to get MS; divide by the ONE MS Within to get F.
  • There is no F on the “Between” row — in a two-factor ANOVA you test its three component parts, not the subtotal.
  • Backwards is the same rules rearranged: SS = MS × df, and N = df Within + cells.

Say it to a friend

“Each effect gets turned into an average amount of variation, and then I ask how many times bigger that is than the ordinary noise. Same noise number every time.”

Key insight

Sharing one denominator is what makes this design efficient. In a one-factor ANOVA on Sound alone, the variation caused by fandom would have been swept into the error term and made MS Within bigger — making the effect you were looking for harder to detect.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Dividing sums of squares to get F. F is a ratio of MEAN squares, so both have to be converted first — and that conversion is what the degrees of freedom are for.

🧪 Demo 5 — complete any two-factor ANOVA table

Check yourself

Why is there no F on the “Between” row?
Because it is only a bookkeeping subtotal here. In a two-factor ANOVA you test its three component parts, not the lump sum.
MS Within = 80. What does that number actually mean?
It is the estimated variance of the scores inside a cell — ordinary person-to-person variation, pooled across all four cells. √80 ≈ 8.9 is the standard deviation of the scores inside a cell.

📑 Judge all three

One column of the F table, three verdicts, three effect sizes

⬇ builds onThe three F values and their df from step 9.⬆ hands upThree decisions about three null hypotheses, and a size for each effect.
  • ▸FAN: F = 4.00 does not reach 4.49, so .05 < p < .10 — H₀ is NOT rejected. No evidence of a main effect of fandom.
  • ▸SOUND: F = 9.00 beats 8.53, so .001 < p < .01 — H₀ is rejected. There IS a main effect of sound.
  • ▸INTERACTION: F = 6.25 beats 6.12, so .01 < p < .025 — H₀ is rejected. There IS an interaction.
🪝 memory hookOne F table column, three verdicts. Then one partial R² per effect.

In words

Each F is compared with the critical value for ITS degrees of freedom. All three here share df(1, 16), so one column of the F table does all the work: 3.05 at p = .10, 4.49 at .05, 6.12 at .025, 8.53 at .01 and 16.12 at .001.

  • The lecture reports R² per effect: .20 for Fan, .36 for Sound, .28 for the interaction.
  • Each R² is SS(effect) ÷ [SS(effect) + SS Within] — a PARTIAL effect size, not SS ÷ SS Total.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • Three F values, three separate decisions. You may reject none, one, two or all three.
  • R² here is partial: the denominator is the effect plus the error, which is why the three do not add to anything meaningful.
  • A non-significant main effect means no EVIDENCE of one — never that the factor is irrelevant.

Say it to a friend

“The music made a real difference, being a fan didn’t on its own, and the two of them interact — the music does much more for fans. The effect sizes say the music is the biggest single piece.”

Key insight

Fandom has no main effect here and yet it controls the entire size of the music’s benefit. That is the lesson the example was built to teach: a factor can be invisible in the margins and decisive in the cells.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Computing R² as SS(effect) ÷ SS Total. That gives .11, .26 and .18 — none of which are the lecture’s numbers. The denominator is SS(effect) + SS Within.

🧪 Demo 6 — significance and size are different questions

Failing to reject H₀ is not evidence that H₀ is true. With n = 5 per cell the test has very little power. The right sentence is that there is no evidence of a main effect of fandom.

🔀 Read the interaction

What it IS, and what the picture tells you

⬇ builds onThe cell means from step 4 and the significant interaction from step 10.⬆ hands upThe description that a significant interaction obliges you to give.
  • ▸For non-fans, Michael beats the weather report by 22 − 20 = 2 points.
  • ▸For fans, Michael beats the weather report by 40 − 18 = 22 points.
  • ▸Two different effects of the same manipulation. That difference IS the interaction.
🪝 memory hook“It depends.” If the honest answer to “does A work?” is “it depends on B”, that is an interaction.

In words

The lecture’s definition: two variables interact if a particular COMBINATION leads to results that would not have been anticipated from the main effects alone. Equivalently — and this is the version to memorise — an interaction means the effect of one variable is different at different levels of the other.

  • In a plot: PARALLEL lines → no interaction. Non-parallel, including crossing → interaction.
  • Read it in whichever direction tells the clearer story — both are the same interaction.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • An interaction is never “A and B both matter”. That is two main effects.
  • Parallel means no interaction. That is the whole rule for reading a plot.
  • A plot SUGGESTS; only the F for the interaction decides whether the non-parallelism is more than noise.

Say it to a friend

“Michael Jackson barely helps if you’re not a fan — two points. If you ARE a fan it adds twenty-two. So whether the music works depends entirely on who’s listening.”

Key insight

Lines that slope do not by themselves prove a main effect, and lines that are separated do not either — a symmetric crossing has both and no main effects at all. What the picture settles cleanly is only the parallel question.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Calling a small wobble an interaction. Real lines are almost never exactly parallel; look for a clear fan or a crossing, not a kink.

Six patterns, and how to read them

Check yourself

A drug lowers blood pressure in men by 10 and in women by 10. Interaction?
No. The effect is the same size at both levels of sex, so the lines would be parallel. There may well be a main effect of sex, but that is a different question.
A revision method adds 8 marks for beginners and takes away 3 for experts. Interaction?
Yes, and a strong one — the effect changes direction, not just size. That is a crossover interaction.

🗣️ Say what it means

The order of interpretation, the cautions, and a real study

⬇ builds onThe three verdicts from step 10 and the interaction description from step 11.⬆ hands upThe sentences that actually earn the marks.
  • ▸ALWAYS PLOT the factor-level means first. The picture shows the shape in one glance.
  • ▸EXAMINE THE INTERACTION FIRST. It decides whether the main effects can be read at face value.
  • ▸WITH AN INTERACTION PRESENT, describe the CELL means — the marginal means no longer tell the whole story.
🪝 memory hookInteraction first, then the main effects. And always draw the picture.

In words

This is where the marks are. The lecture gives an explicit order of interpretation and three cautions, and it closes with a published study that has exactly the shape of the teaching example — a factor with no main effect that nevertheless decides what the other factor does.

  • The advantages: more efficient, smaller samples per condition, less residual variation — and the interaction becomes testable, which no series of one-way ANOVAs can deliver.
  • Dove, Byrne & Bruce (2009): BMI F(2,70) = 8.28 p < 0.01; thinking style F(1,70) = 0.92 p = 0.34; interaction F(2,70) = 5.53 p < 0.01.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • Picture, interaction, then main effects. In that order, every time.
  • A significant interaction does not forbid talking about the main effects — it makes them insufficient on their own.
  • Causal language is still decided by step 3, not by how large any F turned out.

Say it to a friend

“Running the two factors together is cheaper, cleaner and the only way to ask whether they depend on each other. And when they do, I have to describe the four groups, not the two averages.”

Key insight

Dichotomous thinking shows NO main effect in the published study — on its own it does not raise or lower depression — and yet it completely controls whether depression relates to BMI. Report only the main effects there and you would miss the entire finding.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Writing “Michael Jackson raises scores” and stopping. True on average and misleading in practice — it barely raises them for non-fans, and the interaction is exactly what you were asked to notice.

Dove, Byrne & Bruce (2009)

Check yourself

Could you test the interaction by running a one-way ANOVA on Sound for the fans and another for the non-fans?
No. That gives you two separate effects but no test of whether they DIFFER from each other. The lecture states this directly: interactions cannot be tested with a series of one-way ANOVAs.
The Dove study is observational on both factors. What does that cost the conclusion?
Nothing statistically, but everything causally. Neither BMI category nor thinking style was manipulated, so the finding is an association — it cannot show that changing someone’s thinking style would change the link with depression.
